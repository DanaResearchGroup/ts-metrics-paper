\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning models for transition-state (TS) geometry prediction are almost
universally benchmarked by the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of atomic
positions, or by the mean absolute error of the interatomic-distance matrix
(D-MAE), relative to a reference TS. We show that these Euclidean measures are
unreliable proxies for the quantity a practitioner actually needs: whether a
predicted geometry optimizes to a genuine TS at a target level of density
functional theory.

We optimized $520$ interpolation-based guesses for $260$ reactions from the
Transition1x database~[Schreiner M., Sci.\ Data 2022, 9, 779] at the r$^2$SCAN-3c
level. Every geometry-based quality measure -- Kabsch-aligned RMSD, D-MAE,
a reaction-coordinate projection, and cheap semi-empirical descriptors --
saturates in a narrow band of the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic
curve (AUC), $0.77$--$0.84$, for predicting optimization success. About $14\%$ of
low-RMSD guesses fail nonetheless.

Many of those ``failures'' are not failures at all. Of them, $38\%$ converge to a
genuine but different first-order saddle point, and $77\%$ of those lie
lower in energy than the reference (median difference
$\sim 131$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$). These are legitimate alternative TSs that a
reference-matching metric penalizes as errors -- though whether each is an
alternative, stepwise, or unrelated TS is, for these open-shell systems, beyond
the reach of current automated structure perception. Once RMSD is held fixed, no
structural property of the reactant or product distinguishes converging from
failing guesses.

A controlled experiment identifies the cause. Displacing each reference saddle by
a prescribed distance along different directions changes the probability of
recovering it from $33\%$ to $98\%$, so distance alone cannot determine the
outcome. Yet once direction is held fixed as well, the ceiling persists at
AUC $\approx 0.83$. The saturation is therefore an information ceiling, not a
shortcoming of any metric: the best geometric measure we construct, a
parameter-free displacement-weighted RMSD, already operates at that limit.

The conclusions replicate on a second, chemically broader database, where
discrimination at matched guess quality is no better than on Transition1x. They
replicate on a state-of-the-art flow-matching generator too. There, oracle
best-of-$N$ selection roughly triples the deployable single-draw success rate, yet
no reference-free score recovers that gain, so reported best-of-$N$ figures
overstate what a user obtains.

Two consequences follow. First, a cheap GFN2-xTB force descriptor evaluated on the
guess predicts convergence about as well as oracle RMSD and requires no reference
structure -- useful precisely because the ceiling means nothing better is
available. Second, the field needs a change of yardstick. We suggest judging a
predicted TS by whether it optimizes to any valid reactant-to-product
saddle -- its convergence and, where determinable, its connectivity -- rather than
by its RMSD to a single reference, and, for stochastic generators, reporting the
quality of a single draw or a reference-free-selected one rather than an oracle
best-of-$N$. Such surface-based benchmarks would make reported progress reflect
genuine usefulness rather than resemblance to one arbitrary database entry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Predicting transition-state (TS) geometries is a central problem in computational
reaction chemistry~\cite{GrinbergDana2026}, and a rapidly growing set of machine-learning models now
addresses it -- from the early graph-based generator TS-GCN~\cite{TSGCN} through
diffusion models that build the saddle from a 2D graph~\cite{TSDiff} or from 3D
reactant and product structures~\cite{Duan2023}, to the recent optimal-transport
and flow-matching approaches~\cite{ReactOT,GoFlow,RitS}. The more recent of these
are trained on the Transition1x database~\cite{Schreiner2022} and evaluated on
held-out reactions from it. Almost without
exception the headline benchmark for these models is geometric similarity to a
reference TS -- the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of atomic coordinates
after optimal superposition, or the mean absolute error of the
interatomic-distance matrix (D-MAE).

Evaluation practice across this body of work is uniform in kind: the metric is
almost always geometric, either a Cartesian RMSD to the reference saddle or the
D-MAE. Which one a paper reports tracks its training data. Models trained on
Transition1x headline a median RMSD to the held-out reference -- $0.08$~\AA\ for
OA-ReactDiff~\cite{Duan2023} and $0.053$~\AA\ for ReactOT~\cite{ReactOT} -- and
RitS~\cite{RitS} reports the lowest median of any generator evaluated on it. Models
trained on the earlier Grambow and RDB7 reaction sets, such as TSDiff~\cite{TSDiff}
and GoFlow~\cite{GoFlow}, instead report the D-MAE or the coverage of their draws.

Many of these works already look past pure geometry. OA-ReactDiff pairs its
generator with a confidence model that flags unreliable predictions, and both it
and ReactOT report barrier errors of a few kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ with respect to their own
DFT reference barriers -- a measure of geometric fidelity to that reference, not of
thermochemical accuracy. TS-GCN~\cite{TSGCN},
TSDiff, and GoFlow each validate a fraction of their guesses physically, optimizing
them to a saddle and checking that it connects the intended reactant and product.
TSDiff and GoFlow even report that some of their apparent ``errors'' are saddles
lower in energy than the reference.

Yet every headline score is still measured against a single reference saddle, and
that reference is easy to over-trust. The Transition1x saddles were located by
nudged-elastic-band searches at the $\omega$B97x/6-31G(d) level~\cite{Schreiner2022},
and none is guaranteed to be the lowest-energy saddle between its endpoints, or
even the only one. Broader datasets reinforce the point: RDB7~\cite{Grambow2020} and
RGD1~\cite{Zhao2023} span far more chemistry and tabulate full distributions of
barrier heights, so any one collection samples only a slice of the accessible
saddles. Its energetic coverage reflects how the dataset was built, not the
reactions themselves.

The reason the field defaults to these Cartesian measures is practical, not
principled. The physically correct test of a predicted TS -- optimizing it to a
first-order saddle and verifying, via an intrinsic reaction coordinate
(IRC)~\cite{Fukui1981}, that it connects the intended reactant and product --
requires Hessians and reaction-path integrations far too expensive to run for
every prediction during model development. Machine-learned
Hessians~\cite{Yuan2024} and reactive interatomic potentials~\cite{Busk2023}
lower this cost but do not remove the need for a target-level verification. A
cheap geometric distance to a stored reference is an attractive surrogate. Implicit in its use is
an assumption that, to our knowledge, has never been tested directly: that a higher
similarity to the reference implies a more useful guess, i.e., one that is
more likely to converge to a valid TS under a standard saddle-point optimizer.

An analogous reckoning has already taken place in structure-based drug design,
where similarity to a single crystallographic pose was long the standard
benchmark. The PoseBusters analysis~\cite{PoseBusters} showed that deep-learning docking
methods tuned to minimize pose RMSD routinely generate physically invalid
geometries, and the CASF (Comparative Assessment of Scoring Functions)
benchmarks~\cite{CASF2016} established a distinction we return to for the generative
model in Section~\ref{sec:generative}: a model's sampling power -- placing a
good candidate somewhere among its outputs -- is separate from its \emph{scoring
power} -- ranking that candidate first without access to the answer~\cite{Wang2016}. TS prediction, we will show, is
subject to the same split.

Here we directly test that assumption -- that a higher geometric similarity to the
reference implies a more useful guess. We take deliberately simple, geometry-only
predictors, optimize their guesses at a consistent density-functional-theory (DFT)
level, and ask what -- if anything -- about a guess predicts whether it converges to
a valid saddle, drawing conclusions only from quantities a practitioner can compute
without already knowing the answer. Beneath the choice of error metric lies the
following question: when the reference is only one of the many valid saddles a
reaction can admit, does a guess's resemblance to that single structure tell us
whether it is useful at all?

The field has thus already begun to look past pure geometry; what it lacks is a
demonstration of whether geometric similarity is merely incomplete or
fundamentally bounded, and of what a criterion should measure once it is.
We provide both -- and, for evaluations that must remain geometric, identify the
strongest such metric: a parameter-free displacement-weighted RMSD that reaches
the ceiling the standard RMSD and D-MAE fall short of.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data and reference}
We used the Transition1x database~\cite{Schreiner2022} of reactant, product and
TS geometries (originally at the $\omega$B97x/6-31G(d) level). To
obtain an internally consistent reference at our working level, we re-optimized
each Transition1x TS at the r$^2$SCAN-3c composite DFT
level~\cite{Grimme2021} (ORCA~6.0.0~\cite{Neese2025}). The set comprised $260$
reactions ($520$ guesses); $259/260$ reference saddles converged, all with exactly
one imaginary vibrational frequency, confirming genuine first-order saddle points.
Reactants and products were likewise optimized at r$^2$SCAN-3c to
define the minima used for connectivity. Areas under the curve
(Tables~\ref{tab:weights} and~\ref{tab:auc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}) are reported
on the $200$-reaction batch ($400$ guesses) for which the complete descriptor set,
including the semi-empirical force and energy, was computed; the alternative-saddle census
(Section~\ref{sec:alt}) used all $260$ reactions ($520$ guesses).

To test generalization beyond first-row organic chemistry, we repeated the whole
protocol on a second database, Reaction-QM~\cite{ReactionQM2026}. We used only its
density-functional-optimized subset (B3LYP-D3/TZVP), from which we sampled $200$
unimolecular reactions; these contain second- and third-row elements absent from
Transition1x. All geometries were re-optimized at r$^2$SCAN-3c exactly as above, so
the two databases were compared at an identical working level and under an
identical success criterion. Results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:rqm}.

\subsection{Predictors}
Two geometry-only predictors generated the guesses whose fate we studied:
midpoint, the linear interpolation $\tfrac{1}{2}(D^{R}+D^{P})$ of the
reactant and product distance matrices, embedded in three dimensions by classical
multidimensional scaling (MDS); and bond-order, which models the length of
each reactive bond from Pauling's bond-order/bond-length relation and interpolates
the remaining pairs. These are not intended to be competitive with modern
learned models; they are cheap, transparent sources of realistic guesses.

\subsection{Downstream optimization and success}
From each guess we ran a TS optimization with analytic Hessians
(ORCA \texttt{OptTS}) followed by a vibrational analysis. A guess was counted a
\textit{success} if the optimization converged, the result had exactly one
imaginary frequency, and its Kabsch-aligned RMSD to the reference saddle was below
$0.3$~\AA. Optimization cost was recorded as the number of geometry cycles and the
wall-clock time.

\subsection{Metrics and their evaluation}
\label{sec:metrics}
We compared guess-quality measures both requiring the reference TS (Kabsch RMSD;
D-MAE; a change-weighted D-MAE; a projection of the aligned error onto the
reaction coordinate, split into components parallel and perpendicular to it; the
un-normalized root-square deviation; and a reactive-atom-only RMSD) and
deployable descriptors requiring only the guess and the reactant/product
(the GFN2-xTB force norm on the guess, and its energy relative to the endpoints).
GFN2-xTB~\cite{Bannwarth2019} denotes the second-generation geometry-, frequency- and non-covalent-
interaction-parametrized semi-empirical tight-binding method. Every measure used in
this work is defined mathematically in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}.

Because the outcome is binary (\textit{success} or \textit{failure}), we quantified how well a
continuous measure separates the two classes by the area under the
receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC). Intuitively, the AUC is the
probability that a randomly chosen \textit{failure} is assigned a larger error than a
randomly chosen \textit{success}: $0.5$ is uninformative and $1.0$ is
perfect separation. The measure needs no decision threshold and is unchanged by
any monotone rescaling of the metric.

\subsection{Controlled displacement experiment}
To test whether a metric's ability to separate success from failure is limited by
the metric itself or by the surface, we constructed guesses whose deviation was
fixed by design rather than observed. Each reference saddle was re-optimized at the GFN2-xTB~\cite{Bannwarth2019}
level with the Sella saddle-point optimizer~\cite{Hermes2022}, and the Hessian was
computed there by finite differences. Displacement directions were taken as the imaginary mode, the softest
and stiffest internal vibrations, and an isotropic Gaussian vector. Because Kabsch alignment removes rigid-body motion, the six translation/rotation
modes were projected out of every direction before use. Without this projection, a
mode dominated by rotation produces a displacement an order of magnitude smaller
than its nominal length. The step length was then solved by bisection so that the achieved Kabsch
RMSD equaled the target; the achieved value was stored for every trial and checked
during analysis. The saddle search was repeated from each displaced structure and
counted as a return if it converged within $0.3$~\AA\ of the original xTB saddle.
The displacement experiment used $187$ (Transition1x) and $186$ (Reaction-QM)
reactions, about $3\,200$ trials per database, at GFN2-xTB throughout so that no reference-level
inconsistency entered. Because the isotropic direction was drawn at random, its
seed was derived from the reaction identifier rather than from the process, so
that splitting the batch across cluster jobs could not alter the result.

\subsection{Generative model}
\label{sec:rits-methods}
To test whether a metric's limited ability to separate success from failure is
specific to interpolation-based guesses, we also generated saddles with
RitS~\cite{RitS}, a flow-matching model that predicts a TS from reactant and
product structures. We used the released general checkpoint
(not the Transition1x-specific one), which avoids train--test leakage on our
Transition1x reactions; the reactant and product minima are the r$^2$SCAN-3c
geometries used everywhere else. Because RitS is stochastic, we drew ten
samples per reaction and kept them all. The predicted atom ordering matches the
input, which we verified per reaction. We then optimized two of the ten samples at
r$^2$SCAN-3c under the same success criterion as the other predictors: the first
draw (what a single call returns) and the sample closest to the reference saddle
(the oracle best-of-ten). Reference-free selection among the ten was evaluated with
three scores -- the geometric medoid, the lowest GFN2-xTB force norm, and the
lowest GFN2-xTB energy -- none of which sees the reference.

\subsection{Connectivity}
For each converged alternative saddle we computed the IRC -- the
minimum-energy descent path in both directions -- and
identified the two endpoints by molecular-graph perception -- covalent bond graphs
and graph isomorphism as implemented in RMG~\cite{RMGv3,RMGv4}, following the structure-perception approach of the
Automated Rate Calculator (ARC)~\cite{ARC} -- restricted where possible to the
bonds that change across the reaction so that conformational differences were
ignored. An endpoint was labeled reactant, product, or a distinct intermediate.
Symmetry-equivalent artifacts (energy within $4.8$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ of the reference saddle)
were removed before this analysis.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Geometry metrics information ceiling}
\label{sec:ceiling}
A geometric metric is only as useful as its ability to anticipate the outcome of
an optimization, so we begin by defining that outcome precisely.
Two distinct binary outcomes are available: \textit{convergence}, whether the
saddle search terminated at any
first-order saddle at all, and \textit{success}, the stricter requirement that it
reached the reference saddle (within $0.3$~\AA, with exactly one imaginary
frequency). All AUC values quoted
below refer to \textit{success} unless stated otherwise; discrimination of mere
\textit{convergence} is uniformly weaker (RMSD: AUC $0.733$ against AUC $0.806$ for
\textit{success}),
because a guess may converge perfectly well to the ``wrong'' saddle
(Section~\ref{sec:alt}).

Table~\ref{tab:auc} reports how well each measure predicts optimization failure
($n=400$). Kabsch RMSD is the best single measure at AUC $0.806$; D-MAE is
slightly lower ($0.769$). Every measure -- including the perpendicular
reaction-coordinate error, which carries essentially all of RMSD's signal while
the parallel component (AUC $0.658$) carries almost none -- lands in a narrow
band around AUC $0.80$. The distributions overlap heavily: about $14\%$ of low-RMSD
guesses fail and successes reach large RMSD, so no distance threshold cleanly
separates the classes (Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}).

Two controls sharpen the point. The un-normalized root-square deviation (RMSD
$\times\sqrt{N}$) gives AUC $0.808$, statistically indistinguishable from RMSD:
the $1/N$ size normalization carries no convergence information. A
reactive-atom-only RMSD is \emph{worse} (AUC $0.783$): a hard binary restriction to
the reacting center discards the whole-molecule fit that partly governs which
basin the optimizer enters.

Between these extremes, a continuous weighting that concentrates on the
reaction zone without discarding the rest improves on plain RMSD: in
Table~\ref{tab:weights} the three weighted rows all exceed the ``uniform'' row,
which is plain Kabsch RMSD at AUC $0.806$. Weighting each atom's squared deviation by its
displacement across the reaction, $w_i = \lvert \mathbf{r}_i^{P}-\mathbf{r}_i^{R}
\rvert$ (Kabsch-aligned), needs no free parameter and no explicit reaction center
and reaches AUC $0.839$; exponential decays in graph-bond distance and in Euclidean
distance from the reactive atoms give AUC $0.819$ and $0.820$. Thus the best purely geometric
convergence measure is neither global RMSD nor a reactive-atom mask, but
this motion-weighted RMSD: a parameter-free reweighting that lifts discrimination
above plain RMSD, and well above D-MAE (AUC $0.769$), to essentially the ceiling.
Where a reference-based geometric score must be reported, it is therefore a better
benchmark metric than the RMSD and D-MAE now standard in the literature -- though,
as the rest of this section shows, still a bounded one.

Finally, a
random forest~\cite{Breiman2001} trained on purely deployable features (GFN2-xTB
force and energy plus reactant/product geometry, with no reference TS) reaches
AUC $0.791$ -- matching oracle RMSD without ever seeing the answer.

The limit is also not an artifact of the coordinate system. Repeating the
analysis in redundant internal coordinates -- the representation the optimizer
itself uses -- does not help: a reaction-change-weighted deviation over all
bonds, angles and dihedrals (each expressed as an equivalent atomic displacement)
reaches only AUC $0.590$, because the many soft, periodic dihedral coordinates
flood the metric; restricted to bonds and angles it recovers to AUC $0.740$, still
below Cartesian RMSD. Weighting the coordinate types by the optimizer's own
default initial-Hessian stiffnesses ($0.5$, $0.2$, $0.1$ for stretches, bends and
torsions, respectively) as a harmonic-energy metric does not help either (AUC $0.610$): the
per-coordinate downweighting is far too small to offset how much further the dihedrals
swing. Their typical deviation is some five times a bond's, so after squaring they still
carry over $90\%$ of the summed harmonic contribution. The result is unchanged when the metric is built on ORCA's exact redundant
internal coordinates, parsed from the optimization logs rather than reconstructed
(AUC $0.600$ against $0.590$ for our bond-graph set). Added to the displacement-weighted
RMSD, internal-coordinate deviations raise the cross-validated AUC
from $0.839$ to $0.844$, within the fold-to-fold noise ($\pm 0.02$). Internal
coordinates -- even the optimizer's own DOFs, weighting, and representation --
thus add nothing to the Cartesian metric's power to anticipate whether a guess
converges to a saddle.

The limit is not specific to geometric information either. An electronic
descriptor -- the deviation of the guess's Mayer bond orders~\cite{Mayer1983,Mayer2007}
from the reference, read from the same DFT output -- reaches AUC $0.700$. Added to the
displacement-weighted RMSD, it again raises the cross-validated AUC only within
noise ($0.839$ to $0.844$). Features computed from the
reactant and product alone -- their geometric separation, reaction energy, and
total bond reorganization -- predict convergence more weakly still. The best of
them, the $R{\to}P$ displacement, reaches only AUC $0.700$, and the reaction energy
is uninformative (AUC $0.460$). These describe how demanding a reaction is on average, not
whether a particular guess lies in a saddle basin, and so cannot exceed the
guess-based ceiling.

Crucially, the limit is not an artifact of choosing a single metric. A random
forest over all eight geometric measures (the five geometric rows of
Table~\ref{tab:auc} and the three weighted variants of Table~\ref{tab:weights})
reaches AUC $0.829$, no higher than the single displacement-weighted RMSD, and
adding the deployable xTB force does not improve on it either. Every guess-based
measure we tried -- geometric or semi-empirical, single or ensembled, oracle or
deployable -- lands in a narrow band that ceilings near AUC $0.84$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}). Convergence carries an irreducible unpredictability
from any function of the guess, because the outcome is fixed by the
potential-energy surface, not by the prediction.

We attribute the surprising transferability -- of a semi-empirical force to the
r$^2$SCAN-3c optimization outcome -- to the breadth of the TS basin:
what governs convergence is not sub-\AA ngstr\"om geometric accuracy but whether
the guess lies within the basin of attraction of the saddle, a region tens of
degrees and tenths of an \AA ngstr\"om wide. GFN2-xTB~\cite{Bannwarth2019}
reproduces this coarse curvature and gradient structure well even where it
mis-estimates the barrier itself, so a small semi-empirical residual force is a
faithful indicator that the higher-level optimizer will fall into the same basin.
The force norm thus measures proximity to \emph{a} stationary point on a
correlated surface, which is exactly the quantity that predicts convergence.

\subsection{Alternative saddles among the ``failures''}
\label{sec:alt}
Of $239$ failures, $142$ ($59\%$) did not converge, $92$ ($38\%$) converged to a
first-order saddle other than the reference, and $5$ collapsed to a minimum. For the
alternative saddles, the median energy difference from the reference is
$131$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, and $77\%$ are lower in energy; only $13\%$ lie within
$4.8$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ of the
reference (symmetry-equivalent artifacts). These are bare r$^2$SCAN-3c electronic
energies, and we do not offer them as accurate barrier heights: a composite DFT
method is chosen here for consistent geometries and Hessians at tractable cost,
not for thermochemistry, and no zero-point, thermal, or higher-level correction is
applied. The claim they support is the ordinal one -- that the alternative saddle
is a different stationary point, usually the lower one -- and the gaps involved are
an order of magnitude larger than the errors such a method is expected to make.
A third of what the metric calls
failure is therefore the optimizer locating a genuine, usually more stable,
TS that the reference database simply does not contain. This is
consistent with reports from the generative-model literature itself: TSDiff~\cite{TSDiff}, for
instance, was observed to sample TSs with lower barrier
heights than the reference database, which its RMSD-based evaluation
scores as deviations rather than discoveries. What these saddles are chemically --
and why that question is harder to answer than it looks -- is taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:irc}.

The magnitude matters kinetically: a median discrepancy of $\sim 131$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$
in barrier height maps to a factor of $\sim 10^{23}$ in the rate coefficient at
$298$~K~\cite{Eyring1935}. The estimate rests on two assumptions. It
equates the electronic barrier difference $\Delta E$ with a free-energy difference
$\Delta G^\ddagger$, neglecting differential entropic and zero-point
contributions; and, more importantly, it presumes the two saddles belong to the
same reaction. Where an alternative saddle is instead a step
of a different mechanism, the comparison is between rates of different
reactions and the prefactors need not cancel. Even then, a barrier gap of this
size dwarfs any plausible prefactor variation of a few orders of magnitude: a
benchmark that treats these saddles as errors is not penalizing a small geometric
slip but discarding TSs whose barriers differ by tens of orders of magnitude in
rate -- precisely the regime where getting the right saddle, rather than
one close in RMSD, is decisive.

\subsection{Robustness of the ceiling}
The class overlap of Fig.~\ref{fig:dist} could be an artifact of the setup rather
than a real limit: the predictor might be too weak, the binary outcome too coarse,
or the metric confounded with the reaction itself. Three controls rule out each in
turn.

A better guess generator does not lift the ceiling. The bond-order predictor
marginally outperforms the midpoint baseline (success $56\%$ vs $52\%$; $20.0$ vs
$21.3$ optimization cycles, paired), but the effect is small, consistent with
guess quality not being the limiting factor.

Convergence is binary, but its cost -- the number of geometry-optimization
cycles -- is graded, and offers a second, more forgiving test of the metrics.
Over the guesses that do converge, the geometry measures correlate with the cycle
count only weakly to moderately (Spearman $\rho$: RMSD $0.59$, perpendicular
reaction-coordinate error $0.55$, weighted D-MAE $0.54$, D-MAE $0.51$; the cheap
GFN2-xTB force reaches $0.55$). The same ordering as for success recurs, at a
lower ceiling: a better guess is somewhat cheaper to optimize, but geometry
explains well under half the rank variance in cost. A perfect guess (the reference
TS itself) converges in $\sim 5$ cycles against $\sim 20$ for the interpolated
guesses, so the potential cost saving is large -- yet not predictable from
Cartesian similarity.

Restricting to low-RMSD guesses ($<0.28$~\AA) -- those that geometric similarity
alone would deem good enough -- and re-testing the reactant- and product-derived
features of
Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}, no feature separates the $30$ failures from the $140$
successes (all AUC $0.5$--$0.6$).
Reaction-amplitude features that appear predictive over all guesses (AUC
$0.65$--$0.68$) collapse to noise once RMSD is held fixed -- they predict failure
only by making the guess worse, not independently. The residual failures are
structurally indistinguishable from the successes -- the same class overlap seen
across all guesses in Fig.~\ref{fig:dist}; their cause lies in the
potential-energy surface (competing saddles, basins), not in the reaction's gross
structure.

\subsection{Why the ceiling exists: a controlled displacement experiment}
\label{sec:ladder}
The above analysis indicates a correlational relationship: guesses differ from the
reference saddle in the size and the direction of their displacement at once, so a
metric's failure could reflect a poor metric or a poor predictor. We separate the
two by constructing displacements whose distance is fixed by design. From each reference
saddle -- first relaxed to a GFN2-xTB saddle so that the
experiment is internally consistent -- we displace the geometry by a prescribed
Kabsch RMSD along four directions and repeat the saddle search: the imaginary
reaction mode, the
softest real vibration, the stiffest vibration, and an isotropic Gaussian
direction. Rigid-body components are projected out and the step length is solved
numerically, so every trial sits at the requested distance to better than
$10^{-3}$~\AA; the achieved distance is recorded for every trial and audited.
A trial ``returns'' if the search converges to the same saddle within $0.3$~\AA.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{../bench/plots/ladder.png}
\caption{Controlled displacement from the reference saddle ($187$ and $186$
reactions, ${\sim}3\,200$ trials per database). Every point at a given abscissa
sits at an identical distance from the saddle -- verified to
$10^{-3}$~\AA\ for every trial -- so the vertical spread is due to direction
alone. Error bars are binomial standard errors.}
\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

At a fixed displacement of $0.20$~\AA, the return rate ranges from $33\%$ along
the reaction mode to $98\%$ along the softest vibration on Transition1x, and from
$22\%$ to $96\%$ on Reaction-QM (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}); direction-independence
is rejected overwhelmingly at every displacement in both databases ($\chi^2$ on
$3$ degrees of freedom, $p<10^{-16}$ throughout, reaching $p=6\times10^{-36}$).
The ordering is physically sensible and identical across the two databases: the
reaction mode is the unstable direction and leaves the basin fastest, while the
softest vibration is the most forgiving. This anisotropy is what the topology of a
saddle region predicts -- reaction paths curve, branch, and pass through
valley-ridge inflections~\cite{Maeda2015}, so displacement along the unstable
eigenvector escapes the basin far sooner than an equal step along a soft
orthogonal mode. \emph{Cartesian distance therefore
cannot determine the outcome, because outcomes spanning $33\%$ to $98\%$ share
the same distance.}

Controlling direction as well recovers part, but only part, of the lost
discrimination. Pooled over directions, the AUC for displacement
predicting failure to return is $0.770$ (Transition1x) and $0.757$
(Reaction-QM); restricted to a single direction it rises to AUC $0.828$ and $0.822$.
Direction-blindness thus costs roughly $0.06$ in AUC -- a real
but modest penalty, and far from the whole gap to a perfect predictor. The
residue is the point: even with the distance controlled to $10^{-3}$~\AA, the
direction held fixed, and the reference saddle identical, displacement predicts
return only at AUC $\approx 0.83$. That is essentially the value reached by the
best purely geometric measure on real predictions (displacement-weighted RMSD,
AUC $0.839$, Table~\ref{tab:weights}).

Because these displacements are drawn from a small set of prescribed lengths,
almost every pair of trials is tied in distance; AUC values are
therefore computed with mid-ranks, without which ties are broken arbitrarily and
the statistic becomes meaningless.

The anisotropy could still be an artifact of the semi-empirical surface. We
tested that directly, repeating the experiment at the working level of theory:
$320$ additional r$^2$SCAN-3c saddle optimizations on $40$ Transition1x
reactions, with the displacement directions taken from the reference DFT Hessians
rather than recomputed. The direction effect reproduces. At $0.20$~\AA\ the
return rate is $98\%$ along the softest vibration against $28\%$ along the
reaction mode ($p=3\times10^{-8}$ and $8\times10^{-14}$ at the two
displacements).

The two levels agree quantitatively as well. Compared like for like -- the same
$40$ reactions, the same two displacements -- their AUCs differ by at most
$0.01$: pooled over directions, $0.651$ at r$^2$SCAN-3c against $0.665$ at
GFN2-xTB; within a fixed direction, $0.711$ against $0.722$. The semi-empirical
surface is therefore a faithful proxy here, and the larger xTB sample can carry
the statistical weight. These absolute values are lower than those quoted above,
but only because the comparison rests on two displacements instead of four. That
is the range effect of Section~\ref{sec:rqm} again: restricting the same xTB data
to those two displacements moves the within-direction AUC from $0.828$ to
$0.702$.

Two independent routes therefore arrive at the same number. This is the
mechanism. The ceiling is not an artifact of metric design, nor merely of RMSD's
blindness to direction, nor of noise in the predictors: it survives an experiment
in which all three are eliminated or controlled by construction. What remains is
irreducible variation between reactions in how large a basin their saddle
possesses -- a property of the potential energy surface that no measurement of
the guess geometry can access. Metrics saturate near AUC $0.83$ because that is where
the information in a guess geometry runs out, and the best of them already
operates at that limit.

A corollary bears on how synthetic guesses should be built. Isotropic Gaussian
noise, the natural way to manufacture guesses of a prescribed quality, does not
behave like a typical direction: it tracks the reaction mode arm, the
harshest of the four. Perturbing a reference structure isotropically therefore
understates the success rate a real predictor would achieve at the same nominal
deviation, and is not a neutral surrogate for predictor error.

\subsection{Generalization to a second, harder database}
\label{sec:rqm}
All results above derive from a single database of small first-row organic
reactions. To test whether they are an artifact of that chemistry, we repeated the
entire protocol on $200$ unimolecular reactions drawn from the density-functional
subset of Reaction-QM~\cite{ReactionQM2026}, which extends to second- and
third-row elements (Si, P, S, Cl) and larger systems. Reference saddles behave as
before ($197/200$ converged, all with exactly one imaginary frequency), so
the comparison is not confounded by a poor reference.

Three things change, and one does not (Fig.~\ref{fig:generalization}). First, the predictors degrade sharply
out of distribution: the median guess deviation rises from $0.35$~\AA\ to
$1.02$~\AA, and only $2$ of $400$ guesses fall below $0.3$~\AA\ (against $155$ of
$400$ on Transition1x). Second, downstream success collapses from $54\%$ to
$6\%$, with convergence falling from $73\%$ to $34\%$ and the median cost of a
converged optimization rising from ${\sim}20$ to ${\sim}32$ cycles. Third, the
fraction of converged guesses that reach an alternative saddle rather than the
reference one grows, from $19\%$ to $26\%$: as reactions become more
complex, the reference TS accounts for a smaller share of the
accessible saddles, reinforcing Section~\ref{sec:irc}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{../bench/plots/generalization.png}
\caption{Generalization to Reaction-QM. (a) The predictors degrade sharply out of
distribution; the shaded band is the Transition1x interquartile range of guess
deviation, used to match the two datasets. (b) Downstream success against guess
deviation: at every matched deviation Reaction-QM succeeds less often, so a
threshold calibrated on one database does not transfer to the other. (c) The
apparent improvement in discrimination on Reaction-QM (AUC $0.920$) is an artifact of
its much wider deviation range; restricted to the matched band it falls to
AUC $0.610$, statistically indistinguishable from both the Transition1x value and from
chance. Error bars are binomial standard errors in (b) and $95\%$ bootstrap
confidence intervals in (c). All values in (c) are AUCs.}
\label{fig:generalization}
\end{figure}

What does not change is the central negative result. Taken over the full range,
the AUC for the deviation
measure appears to \emph{improve} to $0.920$, which would suggest the ceiling of
Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} had been broken. It has not. That figure is an artifact
of range: discrimination is trivially easier when the guesses span a wide quality
range ($0.74$--$1.33$~\AA\ interquartile) than a narrow one
($0.25$--$0.54$~\AA). Restricting the Reaction-QM guesses to the Transition1x
interquartile range removes the advantage entirely -- the AUC
falls to $0.610$ ($95\%$ bootstrap confidence interval $0.42$--$0.80$, $n=53$),
statistically indistinguishable from both the Transition1x AUC of $0.730$
($0.66$--$0.81$) and from chance. Geometric similarity to the reference
TS is therefore no more predictive on a harder and chemically
broader database than on the one the conclusion was drawn from; if anything the
mapping from deviation to outcome is itself database-dependent, so a threshold
calibrated on one dataset does not transfer to another.

The one result that does replicate is the predictor ordering: the bond-order
predictor again edges out the midpoint baseline ($7\%$ vs $5\%$ success), the
same direction and the same negligible magnitude as before.

\subsection{The ceiling in a state-of-the-art generative model}
\label{sec:generative}
The predictors studied so far are deliberately simple. A natural objection is
that the ceiling is a property of weak, interpolation-based guesses and would
lift for a modern machine-learned generator. We therefore repeated the analysis
with RitS~\cite{RitS}, a flow-matching TS generator that takes the
reactant and product geometries and emits a predicted saddle. We use the authors'
released checkpoint, which is not trained on Transition1x, so our evaluation
carries no train--test leakage. Two limits of the tool bound its coverage and are
reported rather than hidden: it supports only the elements C, H, N, O, S, F, P
(removing $96$ of the $200$ Reaction-QM reactions), and its bond perception from
Cartesian input fails outright on $16$ of $200$ Transition1x and $7$ of the
remaining $104$ Reaction-QM reactions, leaving $184$ and $97$ reactions.

The first observation is that the generator is stochastic: each call draws
an independent sample, and the ten draws for a single reaction span a median
range of $0.67$~\AA\ on Transition1x (Fig.~\ref{fig:generative}a) -- wider than
the entire spread between the methods compared in the rest of this paper. This
makes the choice of what to report decisive. The single draw a user actually
obtains has a median deviation of $0.82$~\AA\ (Transition1x) and $1.08$~\AA\
(Reaction-QM), no better than the linear-interpolation baseline. The
best of ten draws, selected by proximity to the reference saddle, has a
median deviation of $0.37$ and $0.73$~\AA\ -- a two- to threefold improvement,
and the number a best-of-$N$ benchmark would report.

That best-of-$N$ number is an oracle. Selecting the closest sample requires the
reference TS, which is exactly the unknown the model exists to
predict. The gap it opens is not academic: optimized at r$^2$SCAN-3c under our
success criterion, the deployable single draw reaches the reference saddle for
$16\%$ (Transition1x) and $4\%$ (Reaction-QM) of reactions, while the oracle
best-of-ten reaches it for $44\%$ and $23\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:generative}c;
McNemar $p<10^{-3}$ on both, discordant pairs $54$ vs $2$ and $18$ vs $0$).
Oracle selection roughly triples the success rate -- a gain entirely unavailable
at prediction time.

The obvious question is whether a reference-free score can recover it. We
tried three: the consensus (medoid) sample, which requires no electronic
structure; the lowest GFN2-xTB force, which is the deployable descriptor this
paper recommends elsewhere; and the lowest GFN2-xTB energy. None works. Across
both databases the sample each selects sits at a mean rank of $4.5$--$5.7$ among
the ten (Fig.~\ref{fig:generative}b), against a chance expectation of $5.5$ and
the oracle's $1.0$; the consensus sample is marginally better than chance and the
energy and force selectors are not. The near-threefold best-of-$N$ advantage is
therefore real but structurally inaccessible: it lives in knowing which sample is
closest to an answer one does not have, and no cheap proxy stands in for that
knowledge. The information ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} thus reappears for
a modern generator in a sharper form -- as a selection problem -- and it cautions
that best-of-$N$ scores in the generative-TS literature overstate deployable
performance by roughly a factor of three.

\subsection{The nature of the alternative saddles}
\label{sec:irc}
We attempted to classify each alternative saddle by computing its
IRC at r$^2$SCAN-3c and identifying the two endpoints
(after a short GFN2-xTB relaxation of the frequently truncated IRC endpoints).
Reference-TS controls, whose IRCs must connect their reactant and
product, validated the endpoint extraction ($6/6$). Symmetry-equivalent artifacts
(energy within $4.8$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ of the reference) account for $\sim 13\%$ of the
alternative saddles and were removed.

However, assigning the remaining endpoints to a chemical species proved
not robust. Four reasonable identity criteria -- covalent adjacency
graph, InChI, graph isomorphism as implemented in RMG (as used by the Automated Rate Calculator,
ARC~\cite{ARC}), and canonical SMILES -- returned between
$6\%$ and $91\%$ ``same reaction'' on the identical set of endpoints. Manual
inspection identified three specific algorithmic blind spots, all aggravated by
the open-shell character of many Transition1x species. First, \emph{radical-center
and multiplicity assignment} is not consistent across two geometries of the same
molecule: perception routines placed unpaired electrons on different atoms and
returned different multiplicities for a species and its own relaxed copy. Second,
resonance-structure selection is arbitrary -- a delocalized radical is
represented by one of several equivalent Kekul\'e/resonance forms, and graph
isomorphism then declares two forms of the same species distinct. Third,
path directionality is lost: an IRC endpoint may relax to a conformer or
tautomer of the reactant/product that the reference minimum does not match, and
the two IRC branches occasionally invert, so reactant and product labels swap.
The net effect is that a single species is repeatedly split into spurious
``intermediates'' (many carried a SMILES string identical to the product yet were
flagged distinct; Fig.~\ref{fig:blind}). We therefore \emph{cannot} state with
confidence whether the
alternative saddles are concerted alternatives of the same reaction, genuine
stepwise elementary steps, or saddles of different reactions. This is itself a
finding: for open-shell reaction networks, even the seemingly simple question
``did the guess find the intended reaction?'' is not answerable by current
automated structure perception, and a robust, perception-independent connectivity
criterion is an open problem. What we can state solidly, the foregoing
notwithstanding, rests on energetics alone: the alternative saddles exist, are
usually lower in energy, and are penalized by a reference-matching metric.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{../bench/plots/blind_spots.png}
\caption{Three algorithmic blind spots that make automated species perception
inconsistent for open-shell IRC endpoints, each illustrated by a pair that is
chemically one species yet is repeatedly assigned two identities. \emph{Left/right
within each row}: (top) resonance ambiguity, a delocalized radical represented by
two equivalent forms; (middle) radical-site migration, the unpaired electron
placed on a different atom for two geometries of the same molecule; (bottom)
path-direction inversion, an IRC whose two branches relax to reactant/product
labels that swap. These failures underlie the $6\%$--$91\%$ spread across identity
criteria in Section~\ref{sec:irc}.}
\label{fig:blind}
\end{figure}

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Cartesian distance is a flawed proxy for surface topology.}
RMSD and D-MAE measure proximity to \emph{one} recorded saddle, whereas what a
practitioner needs is that the guess lie in the basin of attraction of \emph{a}
valid saddle connecting reactant and product -- a topological property. The two
notions coincide only when the reference is the unique relevant saddle, which we
find fails roughly a third of the time.

\paragraph{Reference databases are energetically skewed.}
Interpolation guesses routinely optimize to lower-energy saddles ($77\%$ of the
alternative-saddle cases) absent from Transition1x. A geometrically ``wrong''
prediction frequently reaches a more stable TS. Benchmarking
against a single reference rewards a model for reproducing the database's
particular saddle choice rather than for locating a valid, possibly better,
TS -- even though (Section~\ref{sec:irc}) we cannot robustly
determine, for these open-shell endpoints, whether each lower saddle belongs to
the same reaction, a stepwise variant, or a different one.

\paragraph{Local optimizers are Hessian-blind.}
A quasi-Newton saddle-point optimizer only ever sees the local Hessian; from a
given guess it walks to whichever saddle the local curvature indicates, which need
not be the closest in RMSD nor the reference. This is why no function of the guess
geometry predicts convergence beyond AUC $\approx 0.84$: the outcome is fixed by
the surface, not the guess. Our controlled-displacement experiment makes this
concrete -- direction at fixed distance swings the outcome from $33\%$ to $98\%$,
and even the direction-controlled ceiling matches the best real metric -- so the
saturation is an information limit, not a modeling artifact.

\paragraph{For generative models the ceiling becomes a selection problem.}
A stochastic generator can place a good saddle among its samples far more often
than any single draw suggests: for RitS the oracle best of ten roughly triples
the deployable success rate. But choosing that sample requires the reference, and
none of the reference-free scores we tried -- consensus, xTB force, xTB energy --
selects better than chance. This is the sampling-power/scoring-power split that
structure-based drug design formalized a decade ago~\cite{Wang2016,CASF2016}:
generating a good candidate somewhere in the top-$N$ is a different, easier problem
than ranking it first without an oracle, and the gap between them is a general
bottleneck of generative models, not a quirk of RitS. Best-of-$N$ evaluation
therefore measures the wrong quantity: it credits a model for a sample it cannot
identify without the answer, and overstates deployable performance by about
threefold. The honest figure is the expected quality of a single draw, or
of a draw selected by a genuinely reference-free rule.

\paragraph{Toward better objectives.}
The community should (i)~report convergence rate and IRC connectivity rather than
RMSD/D-MAE; (ii)~score against any valid reactant$\rightarrow$product
saddle rather than a single reference; (iii)~for stochastic generators, report
single-draw or reference-free-selected quality rather than oracle best-of-$N$; and
(iv)~explore topological or energy-derivative-free training objectives (for
instance a force-norm or saddle-character penalty) that reward residence in a
saddle basin over proximity to a particular geometry. For a cheap convergence
proxy at scale, a GFN2-xTB force descriptor on the guess is about as informative
as oracle RMSD and needs no reference structure.

\section{Conclusion}
Geometric similarity to a reference TS does not predict whether a
predicted geometry is useful: every geometry metric saturates in a narrow band
below AUC $0.84$,
and a large share of apparent failures are lower-energy saddles the reference
database omits and the metric penalizes. A controlled experiment locates the
cause. At a fixed distance from the saddle the outcome varies from $33\%$ to
$98\%$ depending only on the direction of displacement, so distance cannot
determine convergence; yet once direction is also controlled the ceiling
persists at AUC $\approx 0.83$, and the effect reproduces at r$^2$SCAN-3c. The
saturation is therefore not a deficiency of
any particular metric but a limit on what guess geometry can express: reactions
differ irreducibly in the size of the basin around their saddle. This holds on a second, chemically
broader database: once the comparison is matched for guess quality, geometric
similarity is no more predictive there than on the database the conclusion was
drawn from, and the mapping from deviation to outcome does not transfer between
the two. The same limit constrains a state-of-the-art generative model: its
oracle best-of-$N$ score, roughly triple its deployable single-draw success,
cannot be recovered by any reference-free selection rule, so reported best-of-$N$
figures do not reflect what a user obtains. TS prediction should be
benchmarked by surface behavior -- convergence, and where determinable,
connectivity -- not by Cartesian distance, and generative models by the quality of
a single or reference-free-selected draw rather than an oracle best-of-$N$. That
determining connectivity is itself hard for open-shell reaction networks only
underscores that geometric distance is the wrong yardstick.

\section{Definitions of the metrics}
\label{app:metrics}

Throughout, a structure of $N$ atoms is a set of Cartesian coordinates
$\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$. We write
$\mathbf{X}^{g}$ for the guess, $\mathbf{X}^{\ddagger}$ for the reference saddle,
and $\mathbf{X}^{R}$, $\mathbf{X}^{P}$ for the reactant and product minima, all at
r$^2$SCAN-3c and in a fixed atom ordering.

\subsection{Alignment and the per-atom deviation}
Let $\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})$ denote the Kabsch superposition of
$\mathbf{X}$ onto $\mathbf{Y}$: the rigid motion $\mathbf{x}\mapsto
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{\mathbf{y}}$ whose rotation
$\mathbf{R}\in SO(3)$ minimizes $\sum_i\lVert\cdot-\mathbf{y}_i\rVert^{2}$. The
per-atom deviation of a guess from the reference is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_i \;=\; \bigl[\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{X}^{g};\mathbf{X}^{\ddagger})\bigr]_i
- \mathbf{x}^{\ddagger}_i ,
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\delta} = \bigl(\mathbf{d}_1,\dots,\mathbf{d}_N\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}.
\end{equation}
Because the multidimensional-scaling embedding fixes chirality arbitrarily, the
mirror image $\mathbf{X}^{g}\!\cdot\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1)$ is also aligned and
whichever of the two gives the smaller RMSD is used; for an achiral reaction the
two describe the same saddle. Every Cartesian measure below is built from the
same $\{\mathbf{d}_i\}$, so differences between them are differences of weighting,
not of alignment.

\subsection{Cartesian measures}
\begin{align}
\text{RMSD} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i}\lVert\mathbf{d}_i\rVert^{2}}, \\
\text{RSD (un-normalized)} &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i}\lVert\mathbf{d}_i\rVert^{2}}
 \;=\; \sqrt{N}\,\text{RMSD}, \\
\text{RMSD}_{\mathcal{A}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert}
 \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert\mathbf{d}_i\rVert^{2}} \quad
 \text{(reactive atoms only)} .
\end{align}
The reactive set $\mathcal{A}$ contains every atom whose row of the covalent
adjacency matrix differs between reactant and product,
$\mathcal{A}=\{\,i : \exists j,\ b^{R}_{ij}\neq b^{P}_{ij}\,\}$, with
$\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,N\}$ in the degenerate case $\mathcal{A}=\emptyset$.

The weighted family (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) is
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSD}_{w} \;=\;
\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i w_i\,\lVert\mathbf{d}_i\rVert^{2}}{\sum_i w_i}},
\label{eq:wrmsd}
\end{equation}
with the four weightings
\begin{align}
\text{uniform:}&\quad w_i = 1, \\
\text{graph decay:}&\quad w_i = e^{-g_i/\tau}, \quad
 g_i = \min_{a\in\mathcal{A}} d_{\mathrm{graph}}(i,a), \\
\text{Euclidean decay:}&\quad w_i = e^{-e_i/\tau}, \quad
 e_i = \min_{a\in\mathcal{A}} \lVert\mathbf{x}^{R}_i-\mathbf{x}^{R}_a\rVert, \\
\text{displacement:}&\quad w_i = \bigl\lVert
 [\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{X}^{P};\mathbf{X}^{R})]_i-\mathbf{x}^{R}_i \bigr\rVert .
\label{eq:dispw}
\end{align}
Here $d_{\mathrm{graph}}$ is the bond-count distance in the reactant covalent
graph and $\tau=2$ (bonds, and \AA\ respectively). Only the displacement
weighting~\eqref{eq:dispw} is free of both a decay constant and an explicit
reactive-atom set, which is the sense in which it is called parameter-free; it is
the measure that reaches AUC $0.839$.

\subsection{Distance-matrix measures}
With $D_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert$ (rotation- and
translation-invariant, so no alignment is needed),
\begin{align}
\text{D-MAE} &= \frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i<j}
 \bigl\lvert D^{g}_{ij}-D^{\ddagger}_{ij}\bigr\rvert, \\
\text{D-MAE}_{w} &= \frac{\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\bigl\lvert D^{g}_{ij}-D^{\ddagger}_{ij}\bigr\rvert}
 {\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}},
 \qquad w_{ij} = \bigl\lvert D^{P}_{ij}-D^{R}_{ij}\bigr\rvert ,
\end{align}
the change-weighted form giving near-zero weight to spectator pairs without an
arbitrary active/inactive threshold.

\subsection{Reaction-coordinate projection}
Let $\hat{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}$ be the normalized imaginary-frequency
eigenvector of the reference saddle. The aligned error splits as
\begin{equation}
e_{\parallel} = \bigl\lvert \boldsymbol{\delta}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}\bigr\rvert,
\qquad
e_{\perp} = \bigl\lVert \boldsymbol{\delta}-(\boldsymbol{\delta}\cdot
\hat{\mathbf{v}})\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}\bigr\rVert,
\qquad
e_{\parallel}^{2}+e_{\perp}^{2} = N\,\text{RMSD}^{2} .
\end{equation}
Both are norms of the full $3N$-vector in \AA ngstr\"om, i.e.\ not divided by
$\sqrt{N}$.

\subsection{Internal-coordinate deviation}
Redundant internals $q$ -- bonds $r_{ij}$, angles $\theta_{ijk}$, dihedrals
$\phi_{ijkl}$ -- are enumerated from the union of the reactant and product bond
graphs, so forming and breaking bonds are included. Angular deviations are
converted to an equivalent atomic displacement by the local bond length
(Wilson-$B$ principle), so that all coordinates share units of \AA ngstr\"om:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{q} =
\begin{cases}
\lvert r^{g}_{ij}-r^{\ddagger}_{ij}\rvert, & \text{bonds},\\[2pt]
r^{\ddagger}_{ij}\,\lvert \theta^{g}-\theta^{\ddagger}\rvert, & \text{angles},\\[2pt]
r^{\ddagger}_{kl}\,\lvert \mathrm{wrap}(\phi^{g}-\phi^{\ddagger})\rvert, & \text{dihedrals},
\end{cases}
\qquad
w_{q} = \bigl\lvert q^{P}-q^{R}\bigr\rvert ,
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{wrap}(x)=((x+\pi)\bmod 2\pi)-\pi$ and dihedrals discarded when
either defining angle lies within $20^{\circ}$ of $0$ or $\pi$. The
parameter-free internal metric pools all coordinate types into a single
change-weighted RMS, $\sqrt{\sum_q w_q\Delta_q^{2}/\sum_q w_q}$, in exact analogy
with~\eqref{eq:wrmsd}. The harmonic-energy variant instead forms per-type RMS
values $M_{\mathrm{bond}}$, $M_{\mathrm{ang}}$, $M_{\mathrm{dih}}$ and combines
them as $\sum_t c_t M_t$ with the optimizer's default initial-Hessian stiffnesses
$(c_{\mathrm{bond}},c_{\mathrm{ang}},c_{\mathrm{dih}})=(0.5,0.2,0.1)$.

\subsection{Electronic deviation: Mayer bond orders}
With $B_{ij}$ the Mayer bond order~\cite{Mayer1983,Mayer2007} of the pair $(i,j)$
-- taken from the first Mayer block of the guess optimization log and the last
block of the converged reference, reactant and product -- the oracle electronic
metric is the change-weighted RMS deviation
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{MBO}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\bigl(B^{g}_{ij}-B^{\ddagger}_{ij}\bigr)^{2}}
{\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}}},
\qquad w_{ij}=\bigl\lvert B^{P}_{ij}-B^{R}_{ij}\bigr\rvert ,
\end{equation}
pairs absent from a printed block (order below $0.1$) being treated as $B_{ij}=0$.

\subsection{Deployable descriptors}
These use only the guess and the two endpoints. With GFN2-xTB forces
$\mathbf{F}_i$ and energy $E$ evaluated at the guess geometry,
\begin{equation}
F_{\max} = \max_{i,\alpha}\lvert F_{i\alpha}\rvert,
\qquad
F_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3N}\textstyle\sum_{i,\alpha}F_{i\alpha}^{2}},
\qquad
\Delta E_{\uparrow} = E^{g}-\max\bigl(E^{R},E^{P}\bigr),
\end{equation}
together with $\Delta E_{gR}=E^{g}-E^{R}$ and $\Delta E_{gP}=E^{g}-E^{P}$. The
force norm reported in Table~\ref{tab:auc} is $F_{\max}$. The deployable random
forest additionally uses reference-free geometric features: $\text{RMSD}(g,R)$,
$\text{RMSD}(g,P)$, their absolute difference, $\text{RMSD}(R,P)$, the
corresponding D-MAEs, $N$, and the fraction of interatomic distances of the guess
lying outside the interval spanned by the endpoints,
$\lvert\{(i,j): D^{g}_{ij}\notin[\min(D^{R}_{ij},D^{P}_{ij})-\epsilon,
\max(D^{R}_{ij},D^{P}_{ij})+\epsilon]\}\rvert / \binom{N}{2}$ with
$\epsilon=0.1$~\AA.

\subsection{Success and the area under the ROC curve}
A guess is a \textit{success} if the saddle optimization converges, the resulting
structure has exactly one imaginary frequency, and its Kabsch RMSD to the
reference saddle is below $0.3$~\AA; \textit{convergence} drops the third
condition. For a measure $m$ evaluated on the success set $\mathcal{S}$ and the
failure set $\mathcal{F}$, the reported area under the ROC curve is the
Mann--Whitney statistic with mid-ranks,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\,\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
\Bigl[\mathbf{1}\bigl(m_f>m_s\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\bigl(m_f=m_s\bigr)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the probability that a randomly drawn failure is assigned a larger error
than a randomly drawn success, with ties counted as one half. The tie term is
what makes the statistic meaningful in the controlled-displacement experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:ladder}), where the prescribed step lengths tie almost every
pair of trials. AUC is invariant under any strictly monotone rescaling of $m$ and
needs no decision threshold, which is why measures with incommensurable units --
\AA ngstr\"om, bond orders, forces -- can be compared on one axis.
